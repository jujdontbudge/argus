4. THE TWO WINDOWS, DEFINED AND JUSTIFIED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

4a. THE WARMED WINDOW — PRE-REGISTERED
    Definition: every usable sample with elapsedMinutes >= 30. n = 296.
    Justification (analyze.ts docblock, committed before the run): RSS rises
    during warm-up for reasons that are not leaks — Bun tiers up JIT, each eye's
    first catchUp allocates over the whole seeded backlog at once, the SQLite
    page cache fills, cursors settle at EOF. Measured cold catchUp completes in
    under a minute, so 30 minutes is ~30x the settling time. The data bear the
    premise out: fleet RSS is 491.7 MiB at 0.50 min, peaks at 495.7 at 1.01 min,
    and has fallen to ~143 MiB by minute 20. Discarding 30 minutes discards that
    entire transient and 29 minutes of margin.
    Status: FIXED IN ADVANCE. Estimators, CI, threshold and the restart rule are
    all in commit 47e43a8, which predates sample 1 by 7 seconds.

4b. THE FLOOR METRIC — POST-HOC
    Definition: bucket the usable samples by elapsed time and take, per bucket,
    the MINIMUM of totalRssMiB. This is a floor, not a fit: it asks "how low did
    the fleet ever get back down to in this window", on the theory that a leak
    raises the trough even when the peak is dominated by transient allocation.
    Justification for looking at it at all: peak RSS is noisy here (load spikes,
    catchUp bursts, macOS reclaim), and a trough is a cheap robust statistic.
    Status: POST-HOC. It was invented by the dispatcher DURING the run, after
    seeing the series. It is NOT in commit 47e43a8. It therefore has no standing
    to overturn, qualify, or soften section 2, and I am not letting it. The whole
    point of pre-registering was to make exactly this move unavailable. Its value
    is as EVIDENCE FOR A FUTURE PRE-REGISTRATION: if a floor statistic is worth
    having, it should be written into analyze.ts and a threshold argued for it
    BEFORE the next soak, and then it will mean something.

    Two further reasons to hold it at arm's length even as a candidate:
      - It is not scale-free. Bucket width is a free parameter chosen after the
        fact, and the answer moves with it (see 5b: +0.66 at 15-min buckets over
        the full run, +1.13 at 15-min warmed, +0.75 at 30-min full, +1.03 at
        30-min warmed). A pre-registered version must fix the width.
      - n is tiny. Six 30-minute buckets gives a 6-point regression whose CI is
        [+0.175, +1.328] — nearly an order of magnitude wide.

7. RISING FLOOR, FLAT-ISH PEAK: LEAK OR CORPUS?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The question: is a floor that rises while the peak does not a leak signature, or
is it cache/arena growth tracking a monotonically growing archive — which is what
a working archive looks like?

TEST 1 — does floor/fleet RSS track the corpus better than it tracks time?
It cannot be told apart in this run, and that is a fact about the harness, not a
finding about Argus. Over the warmed window:

    rows          ~ elapsed    Pearson r = 1.000000
    archiveBytes  ~ elapsed    Pearson r = 0.995606
    archiveBytes  ~ rows       Pearson r = 0.995611

The traffic generator adds 29–36 rows per 30-second sample (mean 30.27), so the
corpus IS a linear function of wall-clock, to six decimal places. Consequently:

    totalRss ~ elapsed       r = 0.7687   (Spearman 0.7704)
    totalRss ~ archiveBytes  r = 0.7730   (Spearman 0.7704)
    totalRss ~ rows          r = 0.7688   (Spearman 0.7704)

archiveBytes wins by 0.004 in Pearson and ties exactly in Spearman. That is not
a discrimination; it is three correlations with the same variable wearing three
hats. ANY monotone-in-time quantity would score ~0.77 here. So: THIS RUN CANNOT
DISTINGUISH "leaks per minute" FROM "caches per row". Stated plainly rather than
resolved in favour of the comfortable reading.

TEST 2 — is it just the machine? Load is a real and large confound, and it runs
the wrong way. Median load1 by third of the warmed window was 18.4 / 9.1 / 5.3,
falling as RSS rose (load1 ~ elapsed, r = -0.4264):

    totalRss ~ load1   Pearson r = -0.4005, Spearman -0.7452
    regression         -1.035 MiB of fleet RSS per unit of load1

On macOS this is exactly what the compressor looks like: while the machine is
busy, pages are compressed or evicted and RSS reads low; as pressure lifts they
fault back and RSS reads high. Two controls:

    (a) residual control. Regress fleet RSS on load1, then fit the residual
        against time: +0.9594 MiB/min. Load explains about a fifth of the
        headline slope, not the whole thing.
    (b) like-for-like subset. Restrict to warmed samples with load1 < 10
        (n = 131, spanning minute 34 to minute 180): slope +1.3378 MiB/min,
        r = 0.7777, running 147.2 MiB at 34.0 min to 319.9 MiB at 179.9 min.

At comparable, low load the fleet still roughly doubled. The growth is not a
memory-pressure artifact. Caveat, stated because it matters: 60 of those 131
low-load samples fall in the last 30 minutes and only 3 fall in 30–60 min, so
the subset is not balanced across the run.

TEST 3 — what shape is it? A classic leak is linear. This is convex: flat for an
hour (slope -0.023, CI spanning zero), weakly positive for the next (+0.402, CI
spanning zero), then +3.296 [+2.851, +3.742] in the final 50 minutes. But
archiveBytes grew DEAD LINEARLY throughout (+39,004 B/min, CI ±420, Theil-Sen
+40,440) and rows likewise. A cache sized proportionally to the archive would
therefore grow linearly too — it would not sit flat for two hours and then bend
upward. So the simple "cache tracks corpus" reading does not by itself explain
the shape either.

READ, stated as a read and not as a result:
  - The corpus-tracking hypothesis is NOT supported by this run, but neither is
    it refuted; it is untestable here because rows ≡ time (Test 1). What weakens
    it specifically is Test 3: the corpus grew linearly and the memory did not.
  - The leak hypothesis is not established either. It would have to be a leak
    with a two-hour flat phase, which is unusual, and it lives inside a run whose
    load fell by a factor of four over the same interval (Test 2 argues load is
    not the whole story, not that it is none of it).
  - The most defensible summary: fleet RSS grew, the growth survives a load
    control, it is convex rather than linear, and its cause is not identified.
    Below the pre-registered bar; above "nothing to see".

WHAT WOULD DISTINGUISH THEM — and this is the actionable output of section 7,
since this run cannot:
  1. BREAK THE rows ≡ time COLLINEARITY. Run two arms at different traffic
     rates — e.g. 30 rows/sample and 300 rows/sample — for the same wall-clock.
     If RSS tracks the corpus, the 10x arm grows ~10x faster per minute. If it
     tracks time, both arms grow at the same rate per minute. One extra arm
     settles the entire question and costs nothing but another three hours.
  2. A ZERO-TRAFFIC ARM. Five eyes, no new rows, three hours. Any growth at all
     is a leak by construction — nothing is being cached because nothing arrived.
  3. RUN PAST THE BEND. The convexity is the whole puzzle and the run ended in
     the middle of it. Six hours would show whether +3.3 MiB/min is a new regime,
     a plateau approaching, or an excursion.
  4. INSTRUMENT INSIDE THE PROCESS. totalRssMiB cannot tell a growing SQLite page
     cache from a growing JS heap from an arena that never returns pages. Sampling
     Bun heap stats and the SQLite cache size per eye would separate them
     directly, and is the only one of these four that identifies a CAUSE rather
     than a shape.
  5. PIN THE LOAD. This run shared a machine with #107's live drives and Johan's
     builds. An arm on a quiet machine removes the confound rather than
     controlling for it statistically.

CHECKPOINTS WERE HAPPENING, and this is not an inference from the flat shm — it
is visible directly in walBytes. The series is a sawtooth: 108 separate samples
show walBytes dropping by more than half from the previous sample, spread evenly
across all 180 minutes (first at 2.02 min, last at 178.93 min, roughly one every
100 seconds). Representative drops:

    2.02m   3,312,512 ->   403,792
   55.47m   3,085,912 ->    57,712
   62.06m   2,962,312 ->    41,232
  123.24m   3,052,952 ->   173,072
  178.93m   3,914,032 -> 1,050,632

The WAL repeatedly climbs to just under 4.0 MiB and is then truncated back to
tens or hundreds of kilobytes. 4,185,952 bytes is SQLite's default
wal_autocheckpoint of 1000 pages x 4096 bytes plus frame headers — the ceiling is
the autocheckpoint firing, exactly on schedule, 108 times.

So the flat shmBytes is the RIGHT answer, not a suspicious one. The -shm
WAL-index is allocated in 32 KiB chunks and only grows when the WAL gets large
enough to need more index pages. One chunk for the whole run means the WAL never
outran a single index chunk — which is precisely what regular checkpointing
guarantees. Had nothing forced a checkpoint, walBytes would have climbed
monotonically past 4 MiB and shmBytes would have stepped to 65536 and beyond. It
did neither. The final walBytes of 3,642,112 is simply where the sawtooth
happened to be when the run ended, 11 seconds before teardown; it is not a
backlog.

Verdict: WAL healthy. Bounded, self-truncating, no growth trend (CI spans zero),
and no reader was pinning an old snapshot.

BOUNDED OR HEADING SOMEWHERE BAD? Bounded within any plausible near-term corpus,
and not bounded in principle. At the observed rate a repo would cross 100 ms on
the broad query somewhere around 55k rows, and cross a second at ~470k rows. This
run's traffic generator produced 10,720 rows in 3 hours (~3,600/hour); at that
rate 100k rows is about 25 days of continuous heavy traffic. It is not a problem
now and it will be a problem eventually, which is the definition of something
that belongs in a doc rather than an incident.

DOES IT THREATEN A SPEC CLAIM? No. SPEC.md makes no quantitative claim about
search responsiveness anywhere. What it says is §5.2 "Exposed as `argus search
"<query>"` — a CLI, because a CLI is the one interface all three agents already
share", §10 "`argus search "<query>"` — FTS query over `archive.db`", and §12
"`argus search` answers 'how did we solve X' from any past session of any agent."
Those are capability claims, and 25 ms answers them. The only SPEC number in this
neighbourhood is the 512 MiB per-watcher RSS ceiling, which section 2 shows was
never approached.

So: no SPEC claim is threatened. But SPEC is silent where it should not be. There
is no stated latency budget for `argus search` at all, which means there is
nothing for a future soak to fail against. The recommendation from this section
is to write the constant above into SPEC or an ADR, and pre-register a latency
threshold for the next soak the same way the leak threshold was pre-registered
for this one — because "roughly doubled" was the live read, "17x on one query and
flat on another" is the truth, and the only reason the difference is visible is
that the numbers were kept.

IT FIRED TWICE, BY DESIGN. reap.sh's own header says it is called from three
places on purpose — the driver's EXIT trap, the detached watchdog, and by hand —
because #89 left five watchers orphaned to launchd when teardown lived only in
the process that got killed. Both paths fired here:

  05:08:44Z  driver-trap. The soak loop finished, the driver's EXIT trap ran
             reap.sh, which touched STOP and TERMed the supervisor first (order
             matters: a watcher killed under a live supervisor is a respawn),
             then the sampler, then all five watcher pids as the supervisor last
             recorded them in supervisor-status.json. Traffic had already exited
             on its own. All five TERMs landed on the run's real eye pids:
             7008/7009/7010/7011/7012 — the same five pids from section 6.
  05:08:47Z  stop-file. Three seconds later the detached watchdog (pid 6992)
             noticed the STOP file and ran reap.sh a second time. It found
             supervisor, traffic and sampler already gone and said so. This is
             the idempotency working: a second teardown on an already-torn-down
             run logs "already gone" three times and kills nothing it shouldn't.

THE THREE LEFTOVER KILLS. After its wait loop, reap.sh sweeps anything still
alive whose command line contains this run's unique scratch path — never a bare
`pgrep -f watch`, which would also match Johan's own sessions. The sweep found
three pids:

  6992  The WATCHDOG, which by then had become the stop-file reap.sh — its argv
        is quoted in the log and contains the scratch path, so it self-matched
        the other instance's sweep. This is the two concurrent reaps seeing each
        other. The driver-trap instance is the one that survived to log
        "teardown done", which is why the log has only one "done" line: the
        stop-file instance was killed mid-wait by the driver-trap instance.
        Harmless — it had already finished the work that mattered.

  28297, 28299  Blank argv. `ps -o args= -p` returned nothing for either, which
        means both had ALREADY EXITED in the milliseconds between the `pgrep`
        that listed them and the `ps` that tried to name them. Their pids are
        ~21,000 above the run's own 6986–7012, i.e. they were created near
        teardown time, not at run start — they are not fleet processes. They are
        transient bash/pgrep children of the concurrently-running second reap,
        which inherit that reap's argv (containing the scratch path) and so
        self-match the first reap's `pgrep -f -- "$SCRATCH"`. I confirmed the
        mechanism experimentally rather than asserting it: running two copies of
        the same pgrep-sweep pattern concurrently against a throwaway directory,
        each instance does report the other's script as a leftover. What I could
        NOT do is identify 28297/28299 by name retroactively — they were gone
        before ps ran, and that is exactly why their argv is blank. `kill -KILL`
        on an already-dead pid is a no-op, so this cost nothing either way. This
        is the same `pgrep -f` self-match class of artifact that has produced
        phantom counts here before.

12. WHAT THIS SOAK DID NOT ESTABLISH
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The list matters more than usual here, because section 2's verdict is a
"below threshold" and a below-threshold result is only as strong as the
conditions it was measured under.

 1. NO CHAOS. Nothing was killed, starved, disconnected or corrupted. No disk
    filled, no network partition, no SIGSTOP, no clock jump. The fleet was never
    asked to survive anything.

 2. NO RESTARTS — WHICH MEANS THE RESTART PATH IS UNTESTED. restartLines is 0
    and every eye held one pid for 180 minutes. That is a clean result for
    stability and a total absence of evidence about the supervisor's restart
    logic, its exponential backoff (1s to 60s), or its five-restarts-in-five-
    minutes brake. SPEC describes all three; this run exercised none of them.
    A soak that never restarts anything cannot tell you whether a restart leaks.

 3. ONE MACHINE, ONE REPO, ONE FLEET. A single darwin box, a single scratch repo
    with a single .argus/archive.db, five eyes. Nothing here speaks to multiple
    enrolled repos sharing one supervisor, to a second fleet contending for the
    same store, or to any hardware but this one.

 4. THE MACHINE WAS NOT IDLE, AND THE "LOAD 3–9" CHARACTERISATION IS WRONG.
    This is a correction to the ticket's own framing. Measured load1 across all
    355 usable samples:

        mean 21.3   median 11.9   min 3.23   max 228.48
        fraction of samples with load1 in [3, 9]: 0.37

        by 30-min bucket, mean / median / max / fraction >= 10:
          0–30m    40.2 /  18.0 / 217.5 / 0.71
         30–60m    41.1 /  19.4 / 228.5 / 0.95
         60–90m    15.3 /  14.1 /  47.0 / 0.72
         90–120m   14.5 /  10.0 /  68.9 / 0.49
        120–150m   12.8 /  11.5 /  23.6 / 0.64
        150–180m    4.5 /   4.3 /   7.3 / 0.00

    Only the FINAL 30 MINUTES look like an idle-ish machine. The first hour ran
    at a mean load1 of ~40 with peaks past 228 — #107's concurrent live drives on
    build/evidence-refresh, plus Johan's own work. This is not a footnote: load
    fell monotonically across the run while RSS rose, which is precisely the
    confound section 7 has to control for, and it is the reason section 3's
    convexity cannot simply be read as "the leak turned on at minute 130".
    A quiet-machine arm is needed.

 5. rows ≡ time, SO CORPUS AND DURATION ARE NOT SEPARABLE (section 7, Test 1).
    r = 1.000000. Every "grows with the corpus" and "grows with uptime" claim in
    this document is the same claim. The single most valuable change to the next
    soak is a second traffic rate.

 6. THE RUN ENDED MID-BEND. Fleet RSS was accelerating when sampling stopped:
    -0.02 / +0.40 / +3.30 MiB/min over the three 50-minute thirds of the warmed
    window. Whether +3.3 is a new steady regime, a plateau being approached, or
    an excursion is unknown, because there is no minute 181. A below-threshold
    verdict on a series that is still bending is a weaker statement than a
    below-threshold verdict on a flat one, and it should be read that way.

 7. NO CAUSE WAS IDENTIFIED, ONLY A SHAPE. totalRssMiB cannot separate a growing
    SQLite page cache from a growing JS heap from an allocator arena that never
    returns pages. No in-process instrumentation was sampled.

 8. THREE HOURS IS NOT A DAY. The threshold argument in analyze.ts is explicitly
    about reaching the 512 MiB ceiling "inside a day". Projecting the observed
    per-eye rates from each eye's final RSS:

        eye        warmed rate  -> 512      final-hour rate -> 512
        reviewer     +0.399      18.1 h        +0.955          7.5 h
        digest       +0.388      18.4 h        +0.767          9.3 h
        honcho       +0.258      28.4 h        +0.595         12.3 h
        retro        +0.112      69.9 h        +0.334         23.5 h
        archiver     +0.078     100.9 h        +0.225         34.8 h

    Every one of those is an extrapolation far beyond the measured window and
    none of them is a prediction. They are here to show what the next soak's
    duration should be: a run long enough to falsify them is 12–24 hours, not 3.

 9. NO SEARCH-LATENCY THRESHOLD EXISTED TO FAIL (section 9). Latency was measured
    and reported but nothing was pre-registered about it, so "no SPEC claim is
    threatened" is partly a statement about SPEC's silence rather than about
    Argus's performance.

10. THE FLOOR METRIC IS NOT A RESULT (section 4b). It was invented mid-run. Its
    numbers are recomputed and corrected here so that a future pre-registration
    can use them, and for no other purpose.
